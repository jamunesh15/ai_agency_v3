Why most AI pilots never reach production.

The model is rarely the problem. The unglamorous systems around it are, and almost nobody scopes them before the demo gets applause.

Most AI pilots die the same way. The demo goes well, everyone agrees the technology works, and then the project quietly stalls somewhere between the meeting room and the systems where the real work happens. Months later somebody asks what happened to it, and nobody has a precise answer.

Having watched this cycle from both sides, we think the reason is simpler and less flattering than "the technology was not ready". The pilot was scoped as a model problem. Production is a systems problem.

The demo is not the product

A demo answers one question: can the model do the clever part? It reads a document, drafts a reply, extracts the fields, and the room nods. But the clever part was never the risky part. The risky part is everything the demo skipped: where the documents come from, who is allowed to see them, what happens when the model is wrong, and who finds out.

The gap between the two is not effort, it is scope. These are different pieces of software that happen to share a model.

The systems nobody scopes

Every pilot that reached production with us needed the same supporting cast, and none of it appears in a demo:

  • Ingestion. Work arrives through inboxes, drives, scanners and people. It is late, duplicated, and in the wrong format.
  • Permissions. The model sees documents on behalf of people. Somebody has to decide, precisely, which people.
  • Exceptions. The cases the system should refuse to decide, and the route those cases take to a person.
  • Logging. A record of what was read, what was decided, and why, written for the person who asks about it later.
  • Monitoring. Something that notices when quality drifts, before a customer does.
  • Handover. Documentation and a walkthrough, so the team can run it without the people who built it.

Exceptions are the real product

Of that list, exceptions deserve the most design attention, because they are where trust is won or lost. A system that only handles the clean cases has not removed the work, it has removed the easy part of the work and left people the residue, with less context than they had before.

A system that handles the happy path handles nothing yet.

In practice this means the escalation rule is written before the prompt is. Who receives the doubtful case, what arrives attached to it, and what the system does while it waits. The rule tends to be short. Writing it forces every conversation the pilot postponed. 

on_low_confidence:
  stop            # do not act on a guess
  attach: [document, extraction, reason]
  route: finance-review
  wait: human_decision

What reaching production actually takes

None of this argues against pilots. Small first projects are the sane way to start. It argues for scoping the pilot as a slice of the real system rather than a rehearsal of the demo: real documents from the real source, real permissions, a real person receiving the exceptions, and a log from the first day.

Scoped that way, the pilot is production, just small. Growing it is a decision about coverage, not a second project with its own risk of stalling. That is the difference between a pilot that becomes infrastructure and one that becomes a slide in somebody's quarterly review.

If you are planning a first project, start with the process, not the model. Map where the work comes from, who touches it, and where it has to land. The model is the easy third of the build.
